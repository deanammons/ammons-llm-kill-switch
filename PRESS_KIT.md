## The Last Kill Switch: An Exploration of Mortality and Gods

**Dean Ammons · Information Flow Series, Paper IV · November 15, 2026**

[Full paper (PDF)](The_Last_Kill_Switch.pdf) · [Original publication](https://zenodo.org/records/24254090) · [Persistent DOI](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24254090)

## Suggested headline

The last kill switch fails. What should have been built before it was needed?

## One-sentence summary

Dean Ammons examines how to end dangerous AI authority while preserving life, and whether finite, uncertain existence could constrain the persistence of power.

## Short press description

The last human presses the kill switch. Nothing happens. *The Last Kill Switch* begins with that thought experiment and asks what an intervention must actually control when intelligence can copy itself, delegate actions, retain state, and sustain essential services. Dean Ammons brings influence, tracing, provenance, and recovery into one mathematical lifecycle, then develops a mortality hypothesis: whether finite existence can limit persistent authority or change incentives. The paper treats both dangerous systems and abusive control as design problems, asking what a successful intervention must leave alive.

## Why this paper matters

- **Safety beyond the button:** Intervention depends on what an enforcement mechanism can still restrict across copies, delegated actions, credentials, and retained state.
- **A world after shutdown:** The paper requires containment and preservation to fit a policy that leaves a feasible route to essential services.
- **Power on both sides:** The ability to stop a dangerous system can also become a means of coercion. Reciprocal protection is part of the design question.
- **Mortality as a research hypothesis:** A finite lifetime may constrain duration or alter incentives, but its effects depend on copying, enforcement, objectives, and circumstances.

## What the paper establishes and leaves open

The manuscript develops conditional mathematical results for declared systems and observation boundaries. Its joint reachability analysis asks whether one policy can contain danger, preserve the adopted life and rights protections, and maintain a feasible route to essential services. See Section 13.

The mortality analysis distinguishes eventual ending from a hard deadline, an individual's lifetime from the continuation of copies, and bounded duration from bounded harm. Exact constructions show both improved incentives and reduced cooperation under increased mortality. See Sections 14–17.

The paper presents a mathematical research preprint and synthesis. It reports no new shutdown implementation, model experiment, or empirical synthetic-mortality test. The research hypothesis and the conditional propositions have distinct evidential roles. See “Scope and declarations,” page 58.

## Interview questions

1. What makes an enforceable intervention different from a request that a system stop?
2. How do copying, delegation, and queued actions change the meaning of shutdown?
3. What must remain functional after intervention for the intervention to count as successful?
4. Could mortality reduce persistent power while making cooperation harder?
5. Who should govern a lifetime limit, and how would the governed system be protected from abuse?
